Memorial photo album: gathering photos of a loved one from family and friends

When someone we love dies, their photos are scattered across many lives: an old shoebox at a sibling's house, a colleague's phone, a friend's social media. A memorial photo album brings them together. This gentle, practical guide explains how to collect photos of a loved one from family and friends, ideas for displaying photos at a funeral or celebration of life, and how to keep the album as a memorial.

What is a memorial photo album?

A memorial photo album is a collection of photos — and often stories — that celebrates a person's life. It can be printed, digital, or both. A shared digital album has one big advantage: everyone who knew the person can contribute, including people the family has never met.

Why a shared album helps

  • It gathers photos the family has never seen: from work trips, school days, hobbies and friendships.
  • It takes a task off the family. Instead of collecting and forwarding photos one by one, people add them themselves.
  • It includes those who can't attend the service.
  • It keeps stories, not just images, especially when voices are recorded alongside.

How to collect photos of a loved one

  1. Create an album with the person's name and years: “Margaret Ellis, 1941–2026”.
  2. Write a short note on the upload page: “We're gathering photos and memories of Margaret. Please add any you have.”
  3. Choose privacy. Keep the album hidden so only people with the link can open it, and turn on approval if you'd like to review photos before they appear.
  4. Share the link with the funeral notice, in family chats, and with workplaces, clubs or congregations.
  5. Ask one relative to help elders scan printed photos with a phone.

Guests upload from the phone browser with no app and no account — important when many are elderly or simply exhausted.

Before the service

Share the link when the service details go out. Ask specifically for photos from different stages of life: childhood, youth, family, work, travel, hobbies, the last years. Download the photos you need for printed boards or a slideshow a day or two before the service.

What to write when asking for photos

Many people want to help but don't know how. A short, kind message makes it easy:

  • “We are putting together an album of Margaret's life. If you have any photos of her — from any year — please add them here. No app needed.”
  • “Dad touched many lives. If you have a photo or a story about him, we would love to see it.”
  • “Please share photos of Grandma with us, especially ones we might never have seen.”

Send it with the link and, where possible, a personal note to old friends and colleagues; people respond more to a direct request than to a general post.

Printed memorial photo album vs digital

A printed album is something to hold at the service and keep on a shelf; a digital album collects far more photos and lets distant friends contribute. Most families do both: collect digitally first, then print a curated selection of 40–60 photos.

Funeral and memorial photo display ideas

  • Photo boards — two or three boards by life stage, with simple captions.
  • Timeline table — framed photos arranged from childhood to the final years, with meaningful objects: glasses, a hat, tools, a favourite book.
  • Slideshow — a looped slideshow during the visitation or reception, with gentle music.
  • Memory tree or wall — guests pin printed photos or written memories.
  • Slideshow on a screen — at a celebration of life, guest uploads appear on a screen as people share them.
  • Memory cards — cards on each table inviting guests to write a story, with a QR code to upload photos.

Place a small sign near the guest book: “If you have photos of Margaret, please add them here” with the QR code.

Memorial photo collage and slideshow tips

  • Choose 40–80 photos for a 10–15 minute slideshow.
  • Start with childhood and end with a recent, smiling portrait.
  • Include photos with friends and colleagues, not only family.
  • Add captions with years and places where you know them.
  • Keep the original files; print from the downloaded full-size photos.

Voices and stories, not only photos

As the album owner you can add voice notes next to photos. Ask a sibling to tell the story behind a childhood picture, a friend to describe a trip, or a grandchild to share what they will miss. Over time, these recordings often become the most treasured part of the album. If you'd like guests to leave spoken messages at the service, see voice guest books.

Privacy and dignity

Grief is personal, and not every photo should be public. Each photo can be shown to everyone, kept for friends, or hidden. Approval lets the family decide what appears. Keep the album hidden from public listings so it is reached only through the link you share.

Keeping the memorial photo album

After the service the album remains a place to return to. Relatives can keep adding photos found later — on anniversaries, birthdays, or when clearing out a home. Frequently asked questions

How do I collect photos of a loved one for a funeral?

Create a private album and share its link with the funeral notice. Family and friends upload photos from the phone browser, and you download what you need for boards and a slideshow.

How many photos should be in a memorial slideshow?

Around 40–80 photos for a 10–15 minute slideshow.

What can I display at a funeral besides photo boards?

A timeline table with meaningful objects, a slideshow, a memory wall, memory cards on tables, or a slideshow of the album at a celebration of life.

Can the memorial album be private?

Yes. Keep it hidden so only people with the link can open it, and turn on approval to review photos before they appear.

Can relatives keep adding photos later?

Yes. Uploads stay open after the service, and storage can be extended.
