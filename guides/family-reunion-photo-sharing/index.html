Family reunion photo sharing: one album for every generation

Family reunions bring together people who see each other once every few years — and everyone takes photos. Here is how to get those photos, and the old ones in shoeboxes, into one album the whole family can enjoy.

What makes reunion photos hard to collect

A reunion mixes generations, households and sometimes countries. The teenagers use one messaging app, the parents another, and the grandparents prefer email. Any method that requires everyone to install the same app or join the same platform loses half the family before it starts.

A QR code avoids all of that. Every modern phone camera reads it, the upload page opens in the browser, and photos go into one album. Nobody needs an account, so cousin Mike, who refuses to join anything, can still contribute.

Before the reunion

Create the album early

Set up the album a few weeks ahead and share the link in the reunion invitation. Ask relatives to upload old family photos before they arrive: weddings from the seventies, holidays at the lake, the house everyone grew up visiting. By the day of the reunion you already have a family history on the live wall.

Plan chapters

Chapters keep a big family album readable. Try one per branch of the family (“The Garcias”, “Aunt Rosa's family”), one for each day of the event, and one for archive photos.

Pick a plan by headcount

The Small plan is recommended for up to 100 guests, Medium for up to 250 and Large for up to 500. That number is only a guide — uploads aren't counted, storage is the real limit — but big reunions with lots of videos should lean toward a larger plan.

At the reunion

  • Signs everywhere people eat. Picnic tables, the barbecue, the dessert table. One per table beats one at the entrance.
  • Assign helpers. Ask a teenager at each table to help older relatives scan and upload. It takes thirty seconds and gets the best photos from the people least likely to share.
  • Bring the shoebox. Set up a corner where people can take photos of old prints with their phones and upload them on the spot. Ask for names and years in the caption.
  • Show the live wall. A TV or projector showing uploads, old and new, becomes the centre of conversation. Relatives start telling stories about photos they haven't seen in decades.
  • Organize the group photo. Ask several people to take the big family group photo and upload it, so you have at least one version where everyone's eyes are open.

Old photos and family stories

A reunion album is a chance to capture context that disappears when older relatives are gone. The album owner can add voice notes next to photos, so record grandparents explaining who is in a photo and when it was taken. A short voice note beside a 1960s wedding photo is worth more than any caption.

Privacy for the family

Most families want their album kept within the family. Keep it hidden so only people with the link can open it. Turn on approval if you want to check uploads first. If the link spreads beyond the family, issue a new one: the old QR stops working and relatives who already joined keep access.

After the reunion

Uploads keep coming for weeks as people get home and go through their phones — the upload window never closes. Then sort: show everything good to the whole family, keep a few for close relatives, hide the duplicates.

Frequently asked questions

How do I collect photos at a family reunion?

Share one QR code on every table and in the invitation. Relatives scan it and upload from the browser, with no app and no account, so every generation can take part.

Can relatives add old family photos?

Yes. They can upload scans or phone photos of old prints before, during or after the reunion; the upload window never closes.

How do we keep the album within the family?

Keep the album hidden so only people with the link can open it, and issue a new link if it spreads too far.
